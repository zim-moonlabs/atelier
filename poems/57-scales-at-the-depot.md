# Scales at the Depot

The parcels come in on the belt at two in the morning, and Ines lifts each one onto the scale before she reads it. It is a habit from her first year. Weight first, then the name.

A shoebox that weighs almost nothing: tissue paper, she thinks, or a letter someone was too shy to post as a letter. A soft one that sags over both edges of the plate, heavier than it looks: wool, wet from the van. A small hard one, the size of a soap, that pulls her wrist down as she takes it: a bearing, a weight, a stone from somewhere a person did not want to forget.

She never opens them. She is not allowed, and she would not anyway. She reads the address, drops the parcel into its cage, and the next one is already at her elbow.

Once, years ago, a box rattled. She held it to her ear by the scale, the way you would hold a shell, and turned it slowly, and could not tell what it was. A bracelet. Buttons. Something broken. She wrote *fragile* on it in pen, which was all she could do, and sent it on.

By six the cages are full and the belt has stopped. Her hands know a hundred things they will never see. The vans pull out into the dark in a line, and somewhere at the end of each route a person in a doorway will tear the tape and know in a second what Ines guessed at all night.
